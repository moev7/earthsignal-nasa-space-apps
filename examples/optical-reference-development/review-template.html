Help check the water maps

Look at the same small square on two dates. Tell us what you can see—and when the images don’t give you enough information.

Your answers are human image interpretations. They do not establish ground truth, lake depth, flood danger or the cause of a radar change.

Three simple steps

  1. Find the outlined square. Same 20 m × 20 m area, both dates.
  2. Check visibility, then label. “I can’t tell” is a useful answer.
  3. Record each date. Export a draft to keep your work.
What counts as open water?

Choose “Open water” only if visible open water covers the entire outlined square. Choose “No open water visible” only if you clearly see no open water in that square. If it contains a mix, or you cannot distinguish water from a dark surface, choose “I can’t tell.”

Mixed shorelines, shadows, cloud and unclear images are useful reasons to choose “I can’t tell.” Add a short reason, then record each date before moving on.

“No open water visible” does not mean dry ground. These are dated images, not current conditions. This response definition is not automatically the satellite classifier’s definition.

We hide the classifier’s proposed labels here. That cannot guarantee a reviewer has not seen them elsewhere. This already-inspected development case is not an untouched final validation study.

    Dates, source evidence and scientific limits

    These optical observations are about two days after the 26 June radar observation and one day after the 30 September radar observation. They cannot establish water conditions at the radar acquisition times. A reviewer’s interpretation of these same images is not independent field evidence.

    The 20 m optical classification grid defines each review square; the RGB images retain their native 10 m grid. No radar data is read or resampled. Incomplete, uncertain and not-visible reviews remain separate. This tool publishes no accuracy or adjusted-area estimate.
